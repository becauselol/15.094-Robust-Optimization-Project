\section{Introduction}

Microtransit occupies the gap between private-hire vehicles and scheduled public
transit: it is an on-demand, publicly accessible service that routes shared
vehicles dynamically. A defining design choice for microtransit is the placement
of \emph{virtual bus stops} (VBS) --- fixed candidate pickup and drop-off
locations that structure demand pooling and vehicle routing.

Virtual bus stop (VBS) selection is a key design problem in microtransit
systems because selected stations determine service coverage, passenger walking
access, and the ability to pool demand efficiently. However, station selection
is a strategic decision made before demand is fully realized, while station
activation and OD assignment are operational decisions that can adapt after
demand is observed. A station set that performs well under nominal demand may
therefore be fragile when demand shifts across OD pairs or time periods.

This motivates a robust station selection model. Instead of optimizing only for
a fixed estimated demand vector, we allow OD demand to vary within a budgeted
uncertainty set. The first-stage decision selects a long-term set of candidate
stations, while the second-stage decisions activate stations and assign OD
demand under each realized demand pattern. This timing structure protects
service coverage against plausible demand deviations while avoiding the
excessive conservatism of assuming that all OD pairs simultaneously attain their
worst-case values.

We compare the nominal and robust formulations to evaluate whether robustness
improves out-of-sample service coverage quality. In particular, we study how the
robust model affects assignment quality, high-percentile passenger cost, and the
price paid for robustness.


\section{Model}
\subsection{Nominal Model}

\subsubsection{Decision Variables}
\begin{align*}
	y_j       & \in \{0,1\} &  & \text{build station } j                                \\
	z_{js}    & \in \{0,1\} &  & \text{station } j \text{ is active in scenario } s     \\
	x_{odjks} & \in \{0,1\} &  & \text{assign OD pair } (o,d) \text{ to origin VBS } j,
	\text{ destination VBS } k \text{ in scenario } s
\end{align*}

Walking distance induces admissibility sets.  Let $\Delta(o) \subseteq J$ be the candidate pickup stations within the walking limit of origin $o$, and let $\Delta(d) \subseteq J$ be the candidate dropoff stations within the walking limit of destination $d$.  Define the admissible station-pair set
\[
	A_{od}
	:=
	\{(j,k) : j \in \Delta(o),\ k \in \Delta(d)\}.
\]
Only pairs in $A_{od}$ may be used to serve OD pair $(o,d)$.


\subsection{Nominal objective}

The nominal two-stage objective minimizes expected passenger travel cost:
\begin{equation}
	\min_{x,y,z}
	\sum_{s \in S} \sum_{(o,d) \in \Omega} \sum_{(j,k)\in A_{od}}
	q_{ods}\,
	\underbrace{\bigl(d^{\mathrm{orig}}_{oj} + d^{\mathrm{dest}}_{dk} + \lambda\,c_{jk}\bigr)}_{a_{odjks}}\,
	x_{odjks},
	\label{eq:nominal-obj}
\end{equation}
where $d^{\mathrm{orig}}_{oj}$ is the walking distance from origin $o$ to VBS $j$,
$d^{\mathrm{dest}}_{dk}$ is the walking distance from VBS $k$ to destination $d$,
$c_{jk}$ is the in-vehicle travel time between VBS $j$ and $k$, and $\lambda$
is a weight that converts routing time to the same unit as walking distance.

\subsection{Constraints}

\begin{align}
	 & \sum_{(j,k)\in A_{od}} x_{odjks} = 1             &  & \forall (o,d), s \label{eq:assign} \\
	 & x_{odjks} \le z_{js}, \quad x_{odjks} \le z_{ks} &  & \forall (o,d), (j,k)\in A_{od}, s
	\label{eq:active}                                                                           \\
	 & z_{js} \le y_j                                   &  & \forall j, s \label{eq:build}      \\
	 & \sum_j y_j = l, \quad \sum_j z_{js} = k          &  & \forall s \label{eq:budget}
\end{align}

\subsection{Making the model robust against demand uncertainty}

\subsubsection{Uncertainty Set}

For each OD pair $(o,d)$ and scenario type $s$, let $\overline{q}_{ods}$
denote an upper bound on demand, and let $\overline{Q}_s$ denote an upper bound
on \emph{total} demand in scenario type $s$. The uncertainty set is
\begin{equation}
	\cU_s = \Bigl\{
	q_s :
	\underline{q}_{ods} \le q_{ods} \le \overline{q}_{ods}\ \forall (o,d),\;
	\sum_{(o,d)\in\Omega} q_{ods} \le \overline{Q}_s
	\Bigr\}.
	\label{eq:uset}
\end{equation}

This uncertainty set says two things simultaneously:
\begin{itemize}
	\item each OD pair must remain within its own historically calibrated range;
	\item the aggregate demand in the scenario cannot exceed $\overline{Q}_s$.
\end{itemize}

Observed demand is integer-valued, but the robust inner maximization may still
be solved as a linear program.  The key point is that the uncertainty set
\eqref{eq:uset} is a bounded knapsack-type polytope.  If the bounds
$\overline{q}_{ods}$ and $\overline{Q}_s$ are integral, the inner LP has an
optimal extreme point that is integral as well.  Hence dropping integrality in
the inner maximization does not change the worst-case value, even though the
uncertainty set is written in continuous form.

\subsubsection{Robust Counterpart}

The robust model replaces the nominal objective with
\begin{equation}
	\min_{y,z}
	\sum_{s \in S}
	\max_{q_s \in \cU_s}
	\Phi_s(z_s,q_s),
	\label{eq:robust-obj}
\end{equation}
subject to the first-stage constraints \eqref{eq:build}--\eqref{eq:budget},
where $\Phi_s(z_s, q_s)$ is the recourse value defined below.

\subsection{Recourse value function}

The key modeling point is that $x$ is a \emph{recourse} decision.  For fixed
scenario-active stations $z_s$ and realized demand vector $q_s$, define the
recourse value function
\begin{equation}
	\Phi_s(z_s,q_s)
	:=
	\min_{x}
	\sum_{(o,d)\in\Omega}\sum_{(j,k)\in A_{od}}
	q_{ods}\,a_{odjks}\,x_{odjks}
	\label{eq:recourse-value}
\end{equation}
subject to
\begin{align}
	 & \sum_{(j,k)\in A_{od}} x_{odjks} = 1            &  & \forall (o,d), \label{eq:recourse-assign}                 \\
	 & x_{odjks} \le z_{js},\quad x_{odjks} \le z_{ks} &  & \forall (o,d),(j,k)\in A_{od}, \label{eq:recourse-active}
\end{align}
with $x_{odjks} \ge 0$.  Integrality is not needed here: for fixed $(o,d,s)$
the feasible region is a simplex intersected with bound constraints, so an
optimal extreme point selects a single active station pair.

For fixed $z_s$, this problem decomposes by OD pair.  Define the induced
minimum assignment cost
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		m_{ods}(z_s) :=
		\begin{aligned}[t]
			\min_{x_{od\cdot\cdot s}} \quad
			 & \sum_{(j,k)\in A_{od}} a_{odjks}\,x_{odjks}                               \\
			\text{s.t.} \quad
			 & \sum_{(j,k)\in A_{od}} x_{odjks}=1          &  &                          \\
			 & x_{odjks}\le z_{js}                         &  & \forall (j,k)\in A_{od}, \\
			 & x_{odjks}\le z_{ks}                         &  & \forall (j,k)\in A_{od}.
		\end{aligned}
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:induced-cost}
\end{equation}
Because all demand coefficients are nonnegative and the recourse constraints are
separable across OD pairs, the recourse value can be written as
\begin{equation}
	\Phi_s(z_s,q_s)
	=
	\sum_{(o,d)\in\Omega} q_{ods}\,m_{ods}(z_s).
	\label{eq:recourse-separable}
\end{equation}
For fixed $z_s$, the inner worst-case demand problem in \eqref{eq:robust-obj}
becomes
\begin{equation}
	\max_{q_s \in \cU_s}
	\sum_{(o,d)} q_{ods}\,m_{ods}(z_s)
	\quad
	\text{s.t.}\;
	\underline{q}_{ods} \le q_{ods} \le \overline{q}_{ods},\;
	\sum_{(o,d)} q_{ods} \le \overline{Q}_s.
	\label{eq:inner-max}
\end{equation}
This inner problem will be moved into the constraints through an epigraph
variable and then dualized.

\subsection{Derivation of the robust counterpart}

\begin{equation}
	\min_{y,z,\eta}
	\sum_{s \in S} \eta_s
	\label{eq:robust-epi}
\end{equation}
subject to \eqref{eq:build}--\eqref{eq:budget} and
\begin{equation}
	\eta_s \ge \max_{q_s \in \cU_s} \Phi_s(z_s,q_s)
	\qquad \forall s.
	\label{eq:robust-epi-con}
\end{equation}

Using \eqref{eq:recourse-separable}, each \eqref{eq:robust-epi-con} becomes
\begin{equation}
	\eta_s \ge
	\max_{r}
	\left\{
	\sum_{(o,d)} m_{ods}(z_s)\, r_{ods}
	:
	\underline{q}_{ods} \le r_{ods} \le \overline{q}_{ods}\ \forall (o,d),\
	\sum_{(o,d)} r_{ods} \le \overline{Q}_s
	\right\}
	\label{eq:inner-max-r}
\end{equation}

Dualizing the inner LP in \eqref{eq:inner-max-r} gives
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\eta_s \ge\  &
		\min_{\alpha_s \ge 0,\;\beta_{ods} \ge 0,\;\gamma_{ods} \ge 0}
		\quad
		\overline{Q}_s\,\alpha_s
		+ \sum_{(o,d)} \overline{q}_{ods}\,\beta_{ods}
		- \sum_{(o,d)} \underline{q}_{ods}\,\gamma_{ods}
		\\
		             & \text{s.t.} \quad \alpha_s + \beta_{ods} - \gamma_{ods} \ge m_{ods}(z_s)
		             &                                                                          & \forall (o,d)
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:dual}
\end{equation}

By strong duality, \eqref{eq:dual} is equivalent to the existence of
$\alpha_s \ge 0$, $\beta_{ods} \ge 0$, and $\gamma_{ods} \ge 0$ such that
\begin{align}
	 & \eta_s \ge \overline{Q}_s\,\alpha_s
	+ \sum_{(o,d)} \overline{q}_{ods}\,\beta_{ods}
	- \sum_{(o,d)} \underline{q}_{ods}\,\gamma_{ods}
	 &                                                        & \forall s,
	\label{eq:robust-epi-dual}
	\\
	 & \alpha_s + \beta_{ods} - \gamma_{ods} \ge m_{ods}(z_s)
	 &                                                        & \forall (o,d),\, s.
	\label{eq:robust-con}
\end{align}

To remove the value function $m_{ods}(z_s)$, introduce epigraph variables
$t_{ods}$ and assignment variables $x_{odjks} \ge 0$:
\begin{align}
	 & \alpha_s + \beta_{ods} - \gamma_{ods} \ge t_{ods}
	 &                                                         & \forall (o,d),\, s,
	\label{eq:robust-con-t}
	\\
	 & t_{ods} \ge \sum_{(j,k)\in A_{od}} a_{odjks}\,x_{odjks}
	 &                                                         & \forall (o,d),\, s,
	\label{eq:robust-con-milp}
	\\
	 & \sum_{(j,k)\in A_{od}} x_{odjks} = 1
	 &                                                         & \forall (o,d),\, s,
	\label{eq:recourse-counterpart-assign}
	\\
	 & x_{odjks} \le z_{js},\quad x_{odjks} \le z_{ks}
	 &                                                         & \forall (o,d),\, (j,k)\in A_{od},\, s.
	\label{eq:recourse-counterpart-active}
\end{align}
Because the model minimizes $\eta_s$, \eqref{eq:robust-epi-dual} is tight at
optimality, so $\eta$ can be eliminated from the objective.

\paragraph{Robust counterpart MILP.}
The resulting formulation is
\begin{equation}
	\min_{x,y,z,\alpha,\beta,\gamma,t}
	\sum_s \overline{Q}_s\,\alpha_s
	+ \sum_s\sum_{(o,d)} \overline{q}_{ods}\,\beta_{ods}
	- \sum_s\sum_{(o,d)} \underline{q}_{ods}\,\gamma_{ods}
	\label{eq:robust-counterpart}
\end{equation}
subject to
\begin{align}
	 & \alpha_s + \beta_{ods} - \gamma_{ods} \ge t_{ods}
	 &                                                         & \forall (o,d),\, s,
	\label{eq:robust-con-final}
	\\
	 & t_{ods} \ge \sum_{(j,k)\in A_{od}} a_{odjks}\,x_{odjks}
	 &                                                         & \forall (o,d),\, s,
	\label{eq:robust-con-milp-final}
	\\
	 & \sum_{(j,k)\in A_{od}} x_{odjks} = 1
	 &                                                         & \forall (o,d),\, s,
	\label{eq:recourse-counterpart-assign-final}
	\\
	 & x_{odjks} \le z_{js},\quad x_{odjks} \le z_{ks}
	 &                                                         & \forall (o,d),\, (j,k)\in A_{od},\, s.
	\label{eq:recourse-counterpart-active-final}
\end{align}
plus \eqref{eq:build}--\eqref{eq:budget}, with
$x_{odjks},\alpha_s,\beta_{ods},\gamma_{ods},t_{ods}\ge 0$.
\section{Experiments}

We evaluate the proposed model on operational data from a microtransit system
in Zhuzhou, China.  The raw dataset contains candidate station locations, a
directed routing network between stations, and realized trip requests with
timestamps and origin/destination stations.  Because the full network is
already expensive for repeated robust optimization runs, we also construct a
40-station sampled instance together with a sparser 40-station variant.  The
sampled instance preserves the original OD support restricted to the retained
stations, while the sparse instance further reduces effective OD coverage.
This sparse instance is used for the main model comparison because it preserves
most of the observed order volume while substantially shrinking the OD support
that must be calibrated from limited historical data.

\subsection{Experimental Design}

Our main comparison uses the sparse 40-station instance
(`\texttt{zhuzhou\_data\_40\_sparse\_v3}`).
We optimize on the first three days of April 2025 (April 1--3) and then
evaluate the resulting station plans on the full month of May 2025
(May 1--31).  This split is intentionally data-poor on the calibration side:
in practice, operators often do not observe dense historical demand for the
full OD matrix, especially when demand is highly intermittent at the
OD-pair level.

We compare a nominal model against the robust total-demand-cap model.  In the
sparse study, we fix $l=30$ candidate stations, use a walking limit of
300 seconds, set the in-vehicle time weight to $\lambda=10$, and vary the
number of active stations per scenario as $k \in \{10,15,20\}$.  For the
robust model, we vary the shared calibration quantile
$q \in \{0.90,0.95,0.99\}$ used for both the per-OD upper bounds and the
scenario-level demand cap.

\subsection{Uncertainty-Set Estimation}

The robust model uses four time-of-day scenarios
(morning, afternoon, evening, night).  For each period $s$ and OD pair
$(o,d)$, we aggregate historical requests into daily OD counts.  The lower
bounds are fixed at zero,
\[
	\underline{q}_{ods} = 0,
\]
which matches the implemented calibration routine and reflects the fact that
many OD pairs are absent on a large fraction of days.  The upper bounds
$\overline{q}_{ods}$ are estimated as empirical quantiles of the zero-padded
daily OD counts within each period.  The scenario-level cap $\overline{Q}_s$
is estimated using the same quantile applied to total period demand.  In the
baseline diagnostic plots below, we use the 90th percentile calibration to
illustrate the shape of the uncertainty set; in the sparse comparison, we then
repeat the robust runs for $q \in \{0.90,0.95,0.99\}$.

Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty-summary} summarizes the 90th-percentile calibration
on the two 40-station variants.  The sampled and sparse instances share the
same number of stations and directed segments, but they induce very different
observed OD support.  This matters because the robust calibration routine
imputes missing OD upper bounds after estimating quantiles from the observed
April demand.  As a result, both 40-station variants produce very large
box-to-cap ratios, but the sparse instance starts from much thinner observed OD
coverage in every period.  In both variants, the aggregate cap is binding in
every period, but the sparse instance reaches that regime with substantially
fewer observed OD pairs.

\section{Results}

Both experiments are evaluated on the same out-of-sample test set: all 30,100
May orders from the full-OD \texttt{zhuzhou\_data\_40} dataset (949 distinct OD
pairs).  For the sparse experiment this is a \emph{distributional shift}: the
models were calibrated on a sparsified version of April demand that concentrates
all trips onto 130 of those 949 OD pairs, while the May test set contains the
real demand distribution.  For the sampled experiment the train and test OD
supports both come from the same dataset, so there is no such shift.

May costs are computed by \texttt{scripts/evaluate\_full\_od\_may.jl}, which for
each order finds the minimum-cost feasible station pair $(j,k)$ from the active
stations of the relevant time-of-day period.  April costs are the in-sample
equivalents computed with the same method on the sparse calibration orders.
Uncovered = May orders for which no active station lies within the 300-second
walking limit.

\subsection{Sparse Setting: Motivation}

In practice, demand data coverage is often limited: operators may not observe
sufficient historical requests across the full OD matrix, particularly when
many OD pairs are only sporadically active.  In this situation, the nominal
model is calibrated on a thin subset of OD pairs and may build a station plan
that leaves out-of-sample OD pairs uncovered.  To test this regime, we filter
the April calibration data down to the top 10\% of OD pairs by request volume,
reducing the observed OD support from 389 pairs to 130.  This gives the robust
model a structural advantage: its budgeted uncertainty set can hedge against
OD pairs not seen in calibration, while the nominal model is blind to them.

The nominal model has the lowest mean cost in April (in-sample) for every $k$,
confirming it fits the sparse training data well.  On the full-OD May test,

$k=20$ all models cover every order and mean costs converge to within 30 units

\section{Conclusion}

A budgeted uncertainty set provides a practical way to model uncertain OD demand
without the excessive conservatism of full-support worst-case optimization.  The
budget cap $\overline{Q}_s$ encodes the intuition that demand can shift across
OD pairs but the total system load is unlikely to surge simultaneously, keeping
the robust solution from being overly conservative.

The experiments show that robustness buys coverage.  When calibration data is
dense (the sampled network), all models cover every order and robustness
primarily reduces cost variance rather than improving coverage.  When the
calibration data is sparse, the nominal model builds a station plan concentrated
around the observed high-demand OD pairs and leaves peripheral orders uncovered;
the robust model spreads activations more broadly and achieves complete coverage
at a modest increase in mean cost.

An important observation is that coverage guarantees can alternatively be
enforced through explicit constraints rather than through a robust objective.
Such an approach may be more efficient when the goal is purely to ensure service
availability, since it avoids hedging against worst-case demand levels on OD
pairs that are already well covered.

Future work could explore richer recourse models that better reflect true
microtransit operations --- for example, accounting for vehicle capacity,
rebalancing, and multi-period dynamics.  The current recourse function is
separable across OD pairs and does not capture operational constraints that
couple assignments across orders.  It would also be valuable to identify the
specific demand scenarios --- such as significantly low-demand areas or sudden
spatial demand shifts --- where the robust solution provides the greatest
advantage over a nominal plan.
